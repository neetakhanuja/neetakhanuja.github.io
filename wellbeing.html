research  /  cultural probes

cultural probes to understand students’ mental wellbeing

Students may avoid seeking mental health support because of self-stigma. Earlier studies of student wellbeing used anonymous surveys, which gave numbers, or interviews, which gave stories but were not anonymous.

We designed a cultural probe as an anonymous self-reporting tool. Over six days, university students wrote, scribbled and drew about their perceptions, struggles and ways of coping. We asked how to design such a probe, and how students’ reflections could inform design for their mental wellbeing.

IASDR 2023 pictorial, with Joana Lameiras, Madalena Freire, Hugo Nicolau, Jodi Forlizzi and Valentina Nisi

i.

three kinds of probes

Gaver introduced cultural probes to collect inspiration. Later work used them as information probes, with clearer instructions, and as empathy probes, followed by an interview about what participants made. Ours followed the empathy probe: a kit, then an optional follow-up interview.

ii.

designing the probe

Informal conversations with students and with the university counselling service set four aspects of inquiry: support, privacy, stigma and influences. The probe had to be anonymous, privacy sensitive, lightweight and short.

We chose pen, paper and cards. The kit moved from a booklet to a foldable zine to cards, which cost little to produce and showed one task a day.

iii.

a pilot with four students

Four participants tried the kit, followed by four interviews. Two changes came from it.

  • day seven removedDay 7 asked students how they would feel after failing an exam or missing a deadline. Pilot participants found it very uncomfortable, so the study became six days.
  • any languageThe probe was written in English, but participants felt more comfortable in their own language. A new card said they could answer in any language.
iv.

distribution through proxies

To keep participants anonymous to the researchers, five proxies handed out and collected the kits. Each kit held a unique email ID, so a participant could book an anonymous follow-up interview on Zoom.

  • the studySix days, three researchers, five proxies and ten students aged 22 to 25, each compensated with 10 euros.
  • returnsNine of the ten kits came back. One participant withdrew, and the proxy did not share why.
  • misreadingsStudents filled the “how are you” cards at the start and end of the day, not around the task. Some read “neutral” in the association task as a state of mind.
v.

six envelopes, one a day

An A4 envelope held a welcome card, a thank you card and six small envelopes, one for each day. Every day began and ended with a “how are you” card, so we could see how the task changed a student’s mood.

vi.

what students wrote

Three researchers analysed the kits, two of them fluent in the language students wrote in. We translated, digitised and grouped the insights by each day’s task.

  • universityFriends, knowledge and learning came with stress, anxiety, deadlines, overwork and imposter syndrome.
  • when strugglingStudents distracted themselves, called friends, listened to music or ran. Four of nine said they turned to no one.
  • reaching outTalking to others helped some. One student wrote, “I find it hard to verbalize.”
vii.

filling the probe

The “how are you” cards showed how the tasks felt. Some tasks encouraged introspection: “It has actually calmed me more thinking of all the things I do when I am struggling.” Others were hard to face: “These questionnaires are really anxiety inducing. They make me reflect the things I rather not think about.”

viii.

four sensitizing concepts

Across tasks and students, four areas stood out for designing for students’ mental wellbeing.

  • coping strategiesDistraction, friends, exercise, time to reflect, therapy and diaries.
  • quality of relationshipsFamily, partners and friends were a high priority and shaped wellbeing.
  • university and wellbeingDeadlines, grades, uncertainty and imposter syndrome linked university with anxiety, stress and burnout.
  • facilitating counsellingStudents used counselling, but described stigma, difficulty reaching out and long waits.
ix.

three personas

We combined characteristics that students shared into three personas: Oliver, who is anxious and isolated; Jane, who seeks help but sees mental wellbeing as a taboo topic; and Eric, who has positive mental wellbeing.

x.

crossing personas and concepts

Crossing one or more personas with one or more concepts gives scenarios for design. For example: how technology could make counselling part of the coping strategies these students already use, or how each persona is affected by university pressure and relies on relationships for support.

what we learned

Anonymity let students share personal and sensitive details, which made the personas and concepts possible. The study was limited to ten students from one department, and the kits went out during exam week.

Probes returned in the PhD as the aging reflection probe, with older adults in India.
